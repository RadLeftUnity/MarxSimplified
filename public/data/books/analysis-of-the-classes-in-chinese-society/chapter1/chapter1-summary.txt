Mao Zedong opens with the famous foundational question of revolutionary strategy: "Who are our enemies? Who are our friends? This is a question of the first importance for the revolution." Written in March 1926 against both Chen Duxiu's Right opportunism (which relied solely on the Kuomintang bourgeoisie) and Zhang Guotao's "Left" opportunism (which looked only to urban labor while ignoring the countryside), Mao demonstrates that a revolutionary party can only succeed by conducting a rigorous materialist analysis of the economic status and political attitudes of every social class.

Mao systematically examines six major class groupings in semi-colonial, semi-feudal China:

1. The Landlord Class and the Comprador Class: Wholly appendages of the international bourgeoisie and foreign imperialism, representing the most backward feudal and colonial relations of production. They are irreconcilable enemies of the revolution.
2. The Middle Bourgeoisie (National Bourgeoisie): Representing indigenous urban and rural capitalism, they suffer under foreign imperialist competition and warlord oppression, yet fear the militant rise of the proletariat. Their dream of establishing an "independent" bourgeois state is an impossible illusion in an era polarized between the red banner of the Third International and the white banner of counter-revolution.
3. The Petty Bourgeoisie: Comprising owner-peasants (middle peasants), master handicraftsmen, lower intellectuals (students, teachers, clerks), and small traders, subdivided into three economic strata: a right-wing with a small annual surplus that fears revolution, a numerically large self-supporting middle stratum that remains neutral until the revolutionary tide rises, and a left-wing whose standard of living is falling into debt and distress and who eagerly join the revolution.
4. The Semi-Proletariat: Encompassing the overwhelming majority of semi-owner peasants, poor tenant peasants, small handicraftsmen, shop assistants, and pedlars. Crushed by landlord rent extraction and usurious interest, the poor peasants are "the worst off among the peasants and are highly receptive to revolutionary propaganda."
5. The Proletariat: Although numbering around two million in modern railways, mining, maritime transport, textiles, and shipbuilding (alongside urban coolies and hired rural farm laborers), the industrial proletariat is the most concentrated, most ruthlessly exploited, and most progressive class, making it the leading force of the Chinese revolution.
6. The Lumpen-Proletariat: Landless peasants and unemployed handicraftsmen organized in traditional mutual-aid secret societies, who are brave fighters but prone to destructiveness unless guided by the proletarian vanguard.
