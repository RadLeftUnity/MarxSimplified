In On Practice (July 1937), Mao Zedong presents the dialectical-materialist theory of knowledge to refute the twin subjectivist errors of dogmatism (memorizing Marxist phrases divorced from concrete Chinese conditions) and empiricism (relying on narrow, un-theorized practical routine).

Mao establishes that before Karl Marx, pre-Marxist materialism examined knowledge apart from human social nature and historical development. Marxism places social practice at the center of epistemology: human beings acquire knowledge primarily through material production, class struggle, and scientific experimentation. Dialectical materialism possesses two defining characteristics: its class nature (explicitly serving the proletariat) and its practicality (holding that objective results in social practice are the sole criterion of truth).

Mao traces the dialectical movement of human cognition across two qualitative leaps:
1. From Perceptual Knowledge to Rational Knowledge: In initial practical contact with a phenomenon, people gather sense perceptions and external impressions. As social practice repeats, a qualitative leap occurs in the brain, synthesizing rich perceptual data ("discarding the dross and selecting the essential, eliminating the false and retaining the true") into concepts, judgements, and logical inferences that grasp the totality, internal contradictions, and objective laws of the thing. Denying that rational knowledge depends on perceptual experience is the error of idealist rationalism; thinking that knowledge can stop at surface perception without rising to theoretical synthesis is the error of vulgar empiricism.
2. From Rational Knowledge to Revolutionary Practice: Understanding the laws of the world is only half the task; the decisive leap is applying rational theory back into production, class struggle, and national liberation to actively change the world and verify or correct the theory in practice.

Finally, Mao explains that because objective processes continually advance through internal contradictions, revolutionary leaders must constantly update their subjective knowledge. When thinking lags behind changing objective reality, it produces Right opportunism (die-hards); when thinking outstrips the current objective stage and treats future ideals as immediately realizable fantasies, it produces "Left" adventurism and phrase-mongering. The whole of the Marxist theory of knowledge is summarized in the ascending spiral: "Practice, knowledge, again practice, and again knowledge."
