In this intense, line-by-line engagement with Mikhail Bakunin's 1873 book Statism and Anarchy, Karl Marx delivers one of his most precise explanations of the state, class struggle, and socialist democracy.

Bakunin argues that any state, even a workers' state, inevitably produces domination and slavery, asking skeptically: 'If the proletariat becomes the ruling class, over whom will it rule?'

Marx responds directly: so long as other classes (particularly the capitalist class) continue to exist and resist, the working class must wield governmental power and organized coercion to defend the revolution and transform society.

Marx critiques Bakunin's voluntarism, demonstrating that Bakunin understands nothing of the material economic conditions required for social revolution, believing that a revolution can be decreed anywhere simply through 'will' rather than industrial development.

Marx also outlines the policy of the workers' state toward the peasantry: rather than forcibly expropriating or crushing small peasant farmers, the workers' state must immediately improve their conditions and ease the voluntary, economic transition to cooperative farming.

Finally, Marx clarifies the nature of authority and elections under socialism: when the means of production are socially owned, elections cease to be a tool of political domination by a ruling caste and become technical, administrative functions of cooperative social production.
