There was a time when the women of the poorer classes spent their entire lives within the four walls of the home. In our grandmother's day, all this domestic work was necessary and beneficial; it ensured the well-being of the family. The housewife produced such things as cloth, thread, butter, and candles, which had a value as commodities that could be sold on the market.

But capitalism has changed all this. All that was formerly produced in the bosom of the family is now being manufactured on a mass scale in workshops and factories. The machine has superseded the wife. What was formerly produced in the family is now produced by the collective labour of working men and women in the factories.

The family no longer produces; it only consumes. The housework that remains consists of cleaning, cooking, washing, and mending. These are difficult and exhausting tasks, but this work is different in one important way from the work our grandmothers did: these four tasks are of no value to the state and the national economy, for they do not create any new values or make any contribution to the prosperity of the country.

The individual household is dying. It is giving way in our society to collective housekeeping. Instead of the working woman cleaning her flat, the communist society can arrange for men and women whose job it is to go round cleaning rooms. Instead of the working woman having to struggle with the cooking in the kitchen, communist society will organise public restaurants and communal kitchens.

The working woman will not have to slave over the washtub any longer; she will simply take these things to central laundries each week. Special clothes-mending centres will free the working woman from hours spent on mending and give her the opportunity to devote her evenings to reading, attending meetings and concerts. Communism liberates woman from her domestic slavery and makes her life richer and happier.
