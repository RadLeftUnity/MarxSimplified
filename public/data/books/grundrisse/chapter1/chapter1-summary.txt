In the first chapter (Introduction/Notebook M), Karl Marx establishes his method for analyzing political economy. He begins by refuting the prevailing myths of his era, notably the 'Robinsonades' of Adam Smith and David Ricardo, which imagined that human society began with isolated, independent hunters or fishermen. Marx shows that humans are intrinsically social beings, and production can only occur within a specific social and historical context. To analyze production in isolation is a useless abstraction.

Marx then critiques the rigid separations economists make between production, distribution, exchange, and consumption. While typical economics textbooks treat these as independent spheres, Marx demonstrates that they form an organic whole. Production creates the objects; distribution divides them; exchange parcels them out; and consumption destroys them to satisfy needs. However, production is always the primary, dominating moment that initiates the process, while consumption creates the subjective need that drives future production.

Finally, Marx outlines the correct scientific method of political economy. Rather than starting with a chaotic concept like 'the population,' scientists must move from concrete reality to abstract concepts (like value, labor, and exchange), and then retrace their steps to reconstruct the concrete in thought as a rich totality of many determinations. This method of rising from the abstract to the concrete is the only way for the mind to understand real social relations.
