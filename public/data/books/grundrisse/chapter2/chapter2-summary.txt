In the second chapter (The Chapter on Money), Marx engages in a detailed critique of early French socialist reformers, specifically Alfred Darimon and Pierre-Joseph Proudhon. These reformers believed that the root of all capitalist evil was the monopoly of gold and silver. They proposed that replacing precious metal currencies with 'labor-time chits' or credit tickets would allow workers to receive the full value of their labor and eliminate economic crises, all without changing the underlying private ownership of factories and tools.

Marx refutes this by showing that money is not just an arbitrary measurement tool or a simple accounting receipt. Under a system of private commodity production, money is the necessary, materialized form of exchange value. When individuals produce commodities privately, their labor only becomes social when it is exchanged for money. Therefore, the contradictions of money (like inflation, hoarding, and credit crises) are organic results of commodity production itself. Trying to abolish money while keeping private production is a contradiction; it is like trying to keep a king without a crown. To solve the problems of money, one must revolutionize the system of production.
