In the fourth chapter (On Wages), Marx critiques Frédéric Bastiat's apologetic economic harmonies regarding wages. Bastiat argued that wage labor is a highly perfected form of association because it gives workers a 'fixed and stable' income, shielding them from the business risks that capitalists are forced to take. Bastiat painted wage labor as a harmonious and voluntary agreement where the capitalist provides security in exchange for the worker's effort.

Marx exposes this argument as a historical and logical fraud. Historically, wage labor did not emerge from voluntary agreements or a search for security. It arose from the violent dissolution of slavery, serfdom, and communal property. Workers became wage laborers because they were stripped of their land and tools, leaving them with no choice but to sell their labor to survive. The historical character of wage labor is not stability, but total insecurity and dispossession.

Furthermore, Marx argues that the 'fixity' of wages is a myth. Wages fluctuate constantly with supply and demand, and workers are always vulnerable to crises, machinery replacements, and displacement by divisions of labor. The only fixity the working class has under capitalism is the dependency of having to work for wages to survive.
