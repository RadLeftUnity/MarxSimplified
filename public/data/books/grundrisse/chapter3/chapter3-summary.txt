In the third chapter (The Chapter on Capital), Marx explores the transition from money to capital. He demonstrates that money only becomes capital when it is used to buy living labor-power and consume it in production to generate surplus value. Capital is not just accumulated wealth; it is a social relation of production. It is value that seeks to preserve and expand itself by exploiting living labor.

Marx introduces the critical concept of the 'general intellect' and details how capital transforms the production process. Initially, capital simply takes over existing tools. But as industry develops, it builds massive systems of machinery. Under capitalism, the machine does not liberate the worker: it confronts the worker as an alien power, turning the worker into a mere appendage of the machinery. Science and technology are captured by capital to increase the speed and intensity of work.

However, this automation creates its own contradiction. As production becomes highly automated, the creation of real wealth depends less on direct labor time and more on the general progress of science. Yet, capitalism continues to measure wealth by labor time. This contradiction forces capital to reduce labor time to a minimum while inflating it as the sole source of wealth, ultimately working toward its own dissolution and laying the foundation for a cooperative society where wealth is measured by free, disposable time.
