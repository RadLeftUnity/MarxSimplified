In this 1872 address to the International Working Men's Association, Karl Marx analyzes property in the soil as the original source of all wealth and a decisive question for the working class.

Marx begins by dismantling bourgeois legal and philosophical claims that private land ownership is rooted in 'Natural Right', showing that it originated in brute conquest, which conquerors later sanctified through their own legal codes.

He demonstrates that the economic development of society, population concentration, and the necessity of applying modern machinery, irrigation, and chemistry to agriculture make the nationalisation of land a social necessity. Small-scale peasant farming, while accessible under French bourgeois laws, isolates rural tillers, exhausts soil fertility, and creates an artificial antagonism between peasants and urban industrial workers.

Crucially, Marx rejects the proposal to hand land over to isolated cooperative rural associations, which would merely substitute one exclusive class of private producers for another. Instead, he argues that the soil must be owned by the nation as a whole, providing the material basis for an association of free and equal producers carrying on production under a rational, democratic common plan.
