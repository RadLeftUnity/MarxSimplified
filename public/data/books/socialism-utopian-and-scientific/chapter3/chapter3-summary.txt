Engels analyzes the origins and limitations of utopian socialism, focusing on the pioneering work of Claude Henri de Saint-Simon, Charles Fourier, and Robert Owen. Emerging in the early nineteenth century when industrial capitalism was still developing and the proletariat had not yet matured into an independent political force, these early socialists criticized capitalist misery but sought remedies purely out of the human mind. Believing society could be regenerated through reason, propaganda, and philanthropic model communities, they appealed to universal morality rather than class struggle. While praising their brilliant critiques of bourgeois hypocrisy and early flashes of historical insight, Engels shows why their plans were foredoomed to remain utopian: genuine emancipation cannot be invented from outside, but must arise from the material contradictions developing within capitalism itself.
