In the first part of his 1892 English Introduction, Friedrich Engels examines the roots of modern materialism in British philosophy, tracing its trajectory through Francis Bacon, Thomas Hobbes, and John Locke before its expansion into eighteenth-century France. Engels critiques nineteenth-century agnosticism as a 'shamefaced' form of materialism: agnostics admit that all knowledge arises from sensory perception and that the natural world operates through physical laws, yet arbitrarily claim that ultimate reality or the 'thing-in-itself' is unknowable. Engels refutes this agnosticism through the criterion of human practice and experiment: 'The proof of the pudding is in the eating.' By successfully transforming nature according to our perceptions, humanity proves the objective correctness of scientific knowledge.
